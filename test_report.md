# Sodium-Ion Battery Technology for Electric Vehicles in India: Current State

## 1. Executive Summary

Sodium-ion (Na-ion) batteries are being promoted in India as a lower-cost, safer, and strategically resilient alternative to lithium-ion for mass-market EVs, particularly two- and three-wheelers [1][4][5]. Global commercial scale-up is underway, led by Chinese manufacturers [2][7], but the sources reviewed provide little verified evidence of Indian pilot lines, cell production, or vehicle deployments. Confidence in the India-specific picture is low, and most claims rest on news, advocacy, and social-media snippets rather than primary data.

## 2. Key Findings

### 2.1 Policy and strategic framing
- Commentary argues that India should consider sodium-ion as part of its battery strategy, citing cost projections under which sodium-ion could undercut lithium-ion by 2035 [1].
- Sodium-ion is described as a safer and strategically resilient alternative to lithium-based chemistries, in the context of India's supply-chain exposure [5].
- Industry commentary links sodium-ion to Indian road, climate, and cost conditions, though this is advocacy rather than analysis [3].
- The Indian government has approved a Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for Advanced Chemistry Cell (ACC) manufacturing [30]. The sources do not state whether sodium-ion is eligible under this scheme.
- A separate PLI scheme for grid-scale battery energy storage systems was reported as under consideration [29]. This is not EV-specific.

### 2.2 Market outlook
- A December 2025 report projects India's EV battery demand to rise 14x by 2032, and states that sodium-ion and solid-state chemistries are expected to play a defining role, especially in mass-market two- and three-wheelers [4]. The snippet does not give the underlying model or the sodium-ion share.

### 2.3 Global commercialization (context for India)
- BYD is reported to be approaching 50 GWh of annual sodium-ion production capability as of 2026, with investment accelerating from 2025 [2].
- One source states that around 70 GWh of sodium-ion capacity existed as of 2025 [1]. The snippet is truncated, so it is unclear whether this figure refers to capacity, production, or announcements.
- 2026 is described as a pivotal year, with global production capacity coming online [7].

### 2.4 Indian players
- Rechargion Energy (Pune) is reported to be developing sodium-ion technology with plans for larger-scale cell production [6]. This is only visible in a truncated social-media snippet and is not independently corroborated.
- No other Indian firm (e.g., those named in the coverage gaps) was confirmed in the sources with pilot lines, cell production, or vehicle deployments.

## 3. Evidence Analysis

### 3.1 Technical performance: partial agreement and direct contradictions

**Energy density.** Sources disagree on whether sodium-ion can match lithium iron phosphate (LFP) on energy density:
- A review of sodium-ion cells notes that many sodium-ion cathode materials have specific energy and volumetric energy density comparable to LFP [24].
- A non-peer-reviewed comparison states that sodium-ion has lower energy density, shorter cycle life, and lower efficiency than LFP [23].
- A 2023 Chinese-language study discussed in the sources frames layered oxides as offering higher gravimetric energy density than other sodium-ion materials [12].

The first two sources are not directly comparable: [24] is an academic review that focuses on material-level metrics, while [23] is a general-audience site without methodological detail. The evidence supports the view that sodium-ion is competitive at the material level but not conclusively at the cell or pack level.

**Cycle life.** A vendor infographic claims about 10,000 cycles for lithium and about 8,000 for sodium-ion [27]. This is marketing material from a company with an interest in the comparison, so it should be treated as low-confidence. It is consistent with [23] in suggesting sodium-ion has shorter cycle life, but neither source provides test conditions.

**Low-temperature performance (critical for Indian conditions).** This is the most significant contradiction in the set:
- One source identifies poor low-temperature performance as a limitation of certain battery technologies [14]. The snippet is ambiguous about which chemistry is meant.
- Another states that sodium-ion has better performance at low temperatures [18]. The snippet is truncated.

Neither source addresses high-temperature performance, which is the relevant concern for Indian climate conditions. The coverage gaps correctly flag this as unquantified.

**Comparison methodology.** A commentary from a battery manufacturer argues that cell-level comparisons of LFP and sodium-ion overlook manufacturing scale and system integration [21]. This is a useful caveat: vendor and academic comparisons may use different assumptions about scale, and the cost advantage projected in [1] depends heavily on those assumptions.

### 3.2 Cost and sustainability

- Cost projections suggest sodium-ion could undercut lithium-ion by 2035 [1]. This is a projection, not a measured outcome, and the sources do not quantify the Indian cost basis.
- A gigafactory-scale modeling study benchmarks sodium-ion against LFP/graphite as the primary competing technology on energy density and cost [10]. The snippet does not provide the results.
- On manufacturing emissions, a Chinese study ranks sodium-ion ahead of LFP, which reverses the ranking reported by European researchers [22]. This is a direct contradiction. It likely reflects differences in system boundaries, energy grids, and assumptions, but the sources do not explain the discrepancy.
- A 2019 Indian think-tank report anticipated sodium-ion entering the long-range EV market [8]. This is dated, and its projections have not been validated by the more recent sources.

### 3.3 Maturity

Lithium-ion is described as an established technology, whereas sodium-ion has less commercial history [15]. This is consistent across the academic and commercial sources
