# Sodium-Ion Battery Technology for Electric Vehicles in India: Current State

## 1. Executive Summary

Sodium-ion (Na-ion) batteries are technically viable and moving toward commercialization globally, but the evidence on their status in India is thin. Indian research institutions (CSIR, IISc, IIT Delhi, BARC) are active in research and patenting, and a policy and industry push is underway [4][13][26]. The collected sources do not document any Indian OEM or cell maker deploying sodium-ion packs in electric vehicles, so the Indian picture is one of research and early policy intent, not commercial EV deployment.

## 2. Key Findings

### 2.1 Technology Maturity and Performance

- **Energy density is the central limitation.** Reviews consistently describe sodium-ion chemistries as having lower energy density than lithium-ion, which restricts their use in long-range EVs [9][10]. A 2025 status update reports a range of 95–175 Wh/kg across reported cells, with a mean of 144 Wh/kg and a median of 150 Wh/kg, for both EV and stationary applications [28].
- **Cathode research is advanced.** Prussian white and layered oxide cathodes are the most studied families, and the design of cathode materials is an active area [1][29]. The IIT Delhi-linked work on Prussian white cathodes [1] is one of the few sources with an explicit Indian research connection.
- **Anodes are shifting toward hard carbon.** Hard carbon has become the dominant anode material [13][14], though its performance depends heavily on precursor and synthesis conditions [13][15]. Recent work points to a need for scalable, sustainable production routes [15].
- **Commercial activity exists beyond India.** Faradion's journey toward commercialization of high-energy-density sodium-ion cells is documented [7], and China's industrialization of sodium-ion batteries is advancing, with supply chains and hard carbon manufacturing developing rapidly [17].

### 2.2 Indian Research and Institutional Landscape

- A bibliometric study concludes that CSIR laboratories are expected to play a key role in Indian sodium-ion development, and that partnerships with IITs and BARC could accelerate applied research, particularly on cathodes and solid-state electrolytes [4].
- A review of Na-ion research in India identifies a leading research system followed by CSIR and IISc [11]. The snippet is truncated, so the full ranking is not verifiable from the collected material.
- Research funding from CSIR and collaboration with IIT Delhi appear in published work [24], though this confirms research support rather than industrial transfer.

### 2.3 Policy and Commercialization Signals

- A Department of Science and Technology (DST) announcement describes a new MoU intended to help commercialize sodium-ion battery technology [26]. Only the headline is available in the collected material, so the MoU's partners, scope, and funding cannot be confirmed.
- A 2026 study on policy-driven strategies for sodium-ion adoption in India highlights the need for integrated domestic supply chains for cathode and anode materials [13]. This suggests policymakers see supply-chain localization as a precondition for adoption.

### 2.4 Economics and Cost Position

- Sodium-ion is widely presented as a lower-cost, more abundant alternative to lithium-ion, because it avoids lithium and cobalt [5][18]. Lithium-ion cost reductions over the next decade could narrow this advantage [8].
- A techno-economic analysis argues that near-term competitiveness against lithium-ion is challenging and depends on critical mineral prices, starting material prices, and learning rates [2]. This is a more cautious view than the general "cheaper" narrative.
- One source cites a cost range of $40–77/kWh for sodium-ion, attributing it to another study [30]. This figure is secondhand and its basis is unclear.

### 2.5 Supply Chain and Sustainability

- Hard carbon precursors (biomass and related materials) offer a route to lower-cost, regionally sourced anodes. Bamboo-based supply chains are one proposed approach [15].
- Life cycle and social-risk assessments of sodium-ion raw materials are emerging [23][25], indicating that sustainability claims are being examined more rigorously.

## 3. Evidence Analysis

**Points of agreement:**
- All sources recognizing sodium-ion's economic appeal also note its energy-density penalty relative to lithium-ion [2][9][10][28]. The consensus is that sodium-ion is better suited to cost-sensitive or stationary applications than to premium long-range EVs, though the sources do not state this explicitly for India.
- Hard carbon is the anode of choice in most recent reviews [13][14][21].

**Points of tension:**
- **Cost advantage:** Sodium-ion is often framed as cheaper [5][18], yet the techno-economic analysis warns that outright cost advantage over lithium-ion is uncertain in the near term [2]. These views are not strictly contradictory, since one describes potential and the other describes near-term conditions, but the optimism in the simpler framing should be treated cautiously.
- **Timeline:** The industrialization report for China [17] suggests rapid scale-up, while the Indian sources describe an early-stage ecosystem [4][11]. The gap reflects differing national contexts rather than a direct contradiction.

**Strength of evidence:** Most sources are peer-reviewed academic reviews with high-citation counts. Technical claims about energy density and chemistry are therefore reasonably well supported. Indian-specific claims rest on a smaller set of sources, several with low citation counts (for example, [4] and [13] have 2–3 citations), and two (the DST MoU [26] and the policy study [13]) are known only from titles or partial snippets.

## 5. Gaps & Limitations

1. **No evidence of Indian EV deployment.** No source documents an Indian OEM (Tata, Mahindra, Ola, and others) or cell maker (Exide, Amara Raja, Reliance) announcing or deploying sodium-ion packs in vehicles.
2. **Policy details are missing.** The collected sources do not confirm eligibility under PLI for advanced chemistry cells, the National Mission on Transformative Mobility and Battery Storage, FAME II, or PM E-DRIVE. BIS or AIS safety standards for sodium-ion cells are also undocumented.
3. **No manufacturing or pilot-line data.** Capacity, commercialization timelines, and industrial transfer status for CSIR, IISc, or IIT labs are absent.
4. **No Indian pricing or total-cost-of-ownership analysis.** Comparisons with LFP in INR/kWh, and TCO for two-wheelers, three-wheelers, or fleets, were not found.
5. **Thin supply-chain analysis for India.** Import dependence on Chinese cathode and anode materials is not quantified.
6. **Limited recent news coverage.** The sources contain little 2024–2025 reporting on vehicle launches, field performance (cold-weather or cycle-life data in Indian conditions), or certification milestones.
7. **No expert perspectives.** Views from industry or policy experts on adoption timelines in India were not collected.
8. **Source limitations.** Several key sources were available only as abstracts or truncated snippets, which limits confidence in specific claims.

## 6. Conclusion & Confidence Assessment

Sodium-ion technology is technically credible and commercially developing internationally, with clear trade-offs in energy density and a cost advantage that is promising but not yet firmly established [2][28]. In India, the technology is at the research and policy-intent stage: research capacity is substantial [4][11], a commercialization MoU has been announced [26], and supply-chain localization is recognized as a priority [13]. The collected evidence does not show that Indian EVs currently use sodium-ion batteries.

**Overall confidence: Medium for global technology status; Low for India-specific commercial status.**

- *Medium confidence (global technology):* Multiple high-citation peer-reviewed sources agree on energy-density limits, chemistry trends, and the cost narrative.
- *Low confidence (India):* Indian claims rest on a few low-citation studies, a news headline, and truncated snippets. The absence of deployment, pricing, and policy data may reflect search limitations rather than the true state of the sector, so the actual Indian status may differ from what this evidence suggests.

**Recommended next steps:** Verify the DST MoU details and the policy study [13][26] from full texts; search Indian business and automotive press for specific OEM and cell-maker announcements; and review official PLI, PM E-DRIVE, and BIS documents directly.
