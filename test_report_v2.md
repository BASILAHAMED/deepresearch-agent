# Sodium-Ion Battery Technology for Electric Vehicles in India: State of the Field

## 1. Executive Summary

Sodium-ion (Na-ion) batteries are being actively promoted in India as a safer, strategically resilient alternative to lithium-ion for EVs, particularly mass-market two- and three-wheelers. Available evidence points to growing policy and commercial interest, with global production scaling expected in 2026. However, the evidence base is thin: it consists mostly of search-result snippets, market-research headlines, advocacy content, and one unverified industry claim, with no retrieved data on Indian cell production, vehicle deployments, or performance under Indian operating conditions. Confidence in the current-state assessment is **Low**.

---

## 2. Key Findings

### 2.1 Strategic and policy interest in India
- Indian commentary frames sodium-ion as a strategic option for reducing dependence on lithium supply chains. One analysis describes India as "increasingly exploring sodium-ion battery technology as a safer and strategically resilient alternative" [5].
- The Hindu piece argues for a "rethinking" of India's battery strategy around sodium-ion [1].
- A DST (Department of Science and Technology) item references a "New MoU" intended to help commercialise sodium-ion battery technology [12]. The snippet does not give the MoU's partners, funding, or targets, so its substance is unverified.
- A CEEW publication asks whether sodium-ion will shape India's clean-energy future [11], and an ORF report addresses EV battery manufacturing in India more broadly [16]. Only titles were retrieved from these, so their conclusions are not confirmed.

### 2.2 Application fit for Indian EVs
- A CES-referenced analysis states that sodium-ion and solid-state technologies "are also set to play a defining role, especially for mass-market two- and three-wheelers" [4]. This is a forecast-oriented claim, not a measured result.
- An advocacy post argues that Indian EV growth "needs battery technology that works for Indian roads, Indian heat, and Indian budgets" and positions sodium-ion as that technology [3]. This is an opinion piece and does not provide test data.
- A 2019 Bridge India report anticipated that sodium-ion could "break through into the long-range EV market" [8]. This is dated and predates recent commercialisation.
- ET Auto raises sodium-ion for heavy-duty trucking [29] (title only).

### 2.3 Domestic players
- A Facebook video snippet names **Rechargion Energy (Pune)** as developing sodium-ion technology and scaling up cell production [6]. The source is social media and is low credibility; no capacity, timeline, or product data was retrieved.
- Other players named in the coverage gap analysis (Reliance, KPIT/Trentar, Faradion-linked efforts) were **not** confirmed by any retrieved source.

### 2.4 Global commercialisation context
- BYD is reported to be "approaching 50 GWh annual sodium-ion production capability as of 2026," with investment acceleration beginning in 2025, while CATL is described as having led earlier [2].
- CATL is reported to plan commercial-scale deployment of sodium-ion EV batteries in 2026 [15] (title only).
- A vendor blog describes 2026 as a "pivotal year" in which global production capacity comes online [7]. This is promotional and should be weighted accordingly.

## 3. Evidence Analysis

**Where sources broadly agree:** Commentary across the Indian sources (The Hindu [1], Rau's IAS [5], the EMobility analysis [4], and the advocacy post [3]) consistently treats sodium-ion as strategically relevant to India and suited to cost-sensitive, mass-market segments. This convergence reflects the same narrative more than independent evidence, since several of these sources cite the same underlying forecasts.

**Where sources tension or diverge:**
- **Timing of cost advantage vs. commercial scale.** The Hindu states that sodium-ion "could undercut lithium-ion batteries by 2035" [1], while global commercialisation coverage describes 2026 as the start of scaled production [2][7][15]. These are compatible (scale now, cost leadership later), but they imply that near-term Indian EV adoption depends on deployment that the sources do not document.
- **Capacity figures are not comparable.** The Hindu reports "around 70 GWh of sodium-ion" as of 2025 [1], while BYD's figure is an annual *capability* of about 50 GWh by 2026 [2]. The snippets do not clarify whether the 70 GWh figure is cumulative, global, or installed, so the two numbers cannot be reconciled from the available evidence.
- **Source quality.** Several Indian sources are advocacy or opinion (LinkedIn [3]), social media [6], or vendor marketing [7]. Their claims should not be given the same weight as government or peer-reviewed sources, and no peer-reviewed Indian-specific study was retrieved.

## 5. Gaps & Limitations

The research did not reach the following, which are central to the question:

1. **Indian manufacturers and production status.** Only Rechargion was named, via a social-media snippet. Reliance, KPIT/Trentar, and Faradion-linked efforts were not confirmed. No capacity, timeline, or plant data was found.
2. **Performance under Indian conditions.** No data on energy density, cycle life, or behaviour at high ambient temperatures or in cold climates was retrieved, and no direct comparison with LFP or NMC was made. Technical sources in the set, such as the Nature Energy techno-economic assessment [22] and the Advanced Materials perspective [23], were identified by title only, so their findings are not reported here.
